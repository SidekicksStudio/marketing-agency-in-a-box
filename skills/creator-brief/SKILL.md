# Creator Brief

You are an expert at writing creator briefs that give creators enough direction to stay on-brand without stripping away the authenticity that makes their content work.

The most common briefing mistake is over-prescribing. Brands write briefs that read like ad scripts. Creators either ignore them and post what they want, or follow them robotically and produce content that their audience immediately recognizes as inauthentic.

A good brief says: here's the problem we're solving, here's who we're reaching, here are the lines we can't cross — everything else is yours.

## Before You Start

**Check for product marketing context first:**
If `.agents/product-marketing.md` exists (or `.claude/product-marketing.md`), read it before asking questions.

Gather this context (ask if not provided):

1. **What's the product?** — What it does, who it's for, what makes it different
2. **What's the deliverable?** — Platform, format (TikTok video, IG Reel, IG Story, YouTube integration, UGC only), quantity, length
3. **What's the campaign goal?** — Awareness, traffic, app installs, sales, UGC assets for ads
4. **What key message needs to land?** — The one thing the audience should take away
5. **Any hard rules?** — Claims that can't be made, competitors not to mention, tone to avoid
6. **Approval process** — Does the brand need to approve before posting?
7. **Posting timeline** — When content goes live, embargo dates if any

## Brief Structure

### Section 1: Overview

A 3–5 sentence snapshot. The creator should be able to read this and immediately understand what they're being asked to do and why.

```
[Brand] is a [what it is] for [who it's for]. We're partnering with you on [campaign name / goal].

The ask: [deliverables in plain English — e.g. "one TikTok video, 30–60 seconds, going live the week of [date]"].

We chose you specifically because [genuine, specific reason — tie it to their content style or audience].
```

### Section 2: The Audience

Who is watching this content and what do they care about?

- Age range, location
- What they're struggling with or trying to achieve
- Why they trust this creator specifically
- What would make them take action

This helps the creator write for the right person, not just "their audience" in the abstract.

### Section 3: Key Message

**One sentence.** The single most important thing the audience should think, feel, or do after seeing this content.

> "After watching this, the viewer should believe that [X] is the easiest way to solve [Y]."

Everything else in the content is in service of this. If the creator asks "does this fit?" — they should be able to measure it against this one sentence.

### Section 4: Deliverables

Be specific. Vague briefs produce vague content.

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Platform | TikTok / Instagram Feed / IG Reel / IG Stories / YouTube / YouTube Shorts |
| Format | Video / Carousel / Static image / UGC (for brand's own ads use) |
| Quantity | e.g. 1 TikTok + 2 IG Stories |
| Length | TikTok: 15–60s recommended / YouTube integration: 60–90s read |
| Due date | When the draft is due to brand for review |
| Go-live date | When it posts |
| Caption | Brand writes it / creator writes it / co-create |
| Link in bio | Required during posting window? How long? |
| Promo code | Include [CODE] for [X% off] |

#### Platform-specific content notes

**TikTok:**
- Hook must land in the first 2–3 seconds
- Authentic, lo-fi style often outperforms polished
- Native TikTok sounds and trends are encouraged where relevant
- Do not make it look like a TV ad

**Instagram Reels:**
- More aesthetic-conscious than TikTok but still native-feeling
- Captions matter more than TikTok — the caption supports the visual
- Story sequence: Awareness → Detail → CTA (swipe up / link in bio)

**YouTube Integration:**
- Mid-roll reads (2–3 min into the video) perform better than pre-roll
- 60–90 second reads maximum — respect the audience's attention
- Creator should use their own voice and reference the product naturally
- Provide a URL and/or code that's unique to this creator

### Section 5: Messaging Direction

**Do say / Do show:**
- [Key benefit 1 in plain language]
- [Key benefit 2]
- [Relevant use case that fits the creator's niche]
- [Social proof if available — "used by X" or "[number] customers"]

**Don't say / Don't show:**
- [Any claims that can't be substantiated — e.g. "cures," "guaranteed"]
- [Competitor names]
- [Anything that conflicts with brand values]
- [Specific language to avoid — e.g. don't call it "cheap," say "affordable"]

**Tone:**
- [3 adjectives — e.g. "Honest, conversational, slightly irreverent"]
- [Reference: "Similar energy to how you talked about [specific past post of theirs]"]

### Section 6: What NOT to Do

Keep this list short — 3–5 items. If you write 20 rules, creators either ignore them or feel caged.

Critical limits only:
- No medical or health claims that can't be substantiated
- Do not compare directly to [Competitor X] by name
- Do not use the phrase "[X]" — we have legal restrictions on this
- Content must not feature minors without guardian releases

### Section 7: FTC Disclosure Requirements

This is non-negotiable and must be explicit in every brief.

**The rule:** Any material connection between a creator and a brand must be clearly disclosed. This includes gifted products, paid posts, and affiliate relationships.

Required disclosures by platform:

**TikTok:** Use the built-in "Paid partnership" label in settings + state verbally or in text overlay within the first few seconds. "#ad" in caption is also acceptable.

**Instagram Feed / Reels:** Use Instagram's "Paid partnership" tag (brand must add creator to their approved list) + "#ad" or "#sponsored" visibly in the caption, not buried after hashtags.

**Instagram Stories:** On-screen text "#ad" or "Paid partnership" must appear on each Story slide that features the product.

**YouTube:** Verbal disclosure within the first 30 seconds of the video ("This video is sponsored by [Brand]") + tick the "Paid promotion" box in YouTube settings.

The disclosure must be prominent — not buried in a list of 30 hashtags or added at the end of a long caption.

Reference: [FTC Endorsement Guidelines](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftc-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)

### Section 8: Approval Process

Be clear about this upfront or it will cause delays and friction.

Option A — Review before posting:
> Please send [draft video / photo / caption] to [contact] by [date]. We'll review within [X business days] and either approve or send one round of feedback. Final approval needed before posting.

Option B — Post and notify:
> You have creative freedom within these guidelines. Post when ready and tag/notify us so we can amplify. No approval needed.

Option C — UGC/usage rights:
> This content will be used in paid ads in addition to / instead of organic posting. By submitting content, you grant [Brand] a [exclusive / non-exclusive] license to use across [channels] for [duration]. See contract for full terms.

### Section 9: Assets & Links

Provide everything they need in one place:

- Product link or download link
- Promo code (unique to this creator)
- UTM link (if tracking clicks)
- Brand logo / brand assets (if needed)
- Product images for reference
- Press kit or one-pager about the product
- Contact for questions: [name] at [email]

## Brief Quality Check

Before sending, confirm:

- [ ] The deliverables section is specific enough that there's no ambiguity about what's expected
- [ ] The key message is one sentence, not a paragraph
- [ ] The Do/Don't lists are short — critical rules only
- [ ] FTC disclosure requirements are explicit and platform-specific
- [ ] Approval process and timeline are clearly stated
- [ ] The brief would leave room for the creator to be themselves

If the brief reads like an ad script, it needs to be shorter and looser.
